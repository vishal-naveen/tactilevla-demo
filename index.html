Six hobby servos and a 3D printer.

The SO-101 is an open-source arm: printed links, six Feetech STS3215 servos, two USB cameras. The published tactile-VLA work runs on research arms that cost around $30,000. This setup costs under $300 — roughly a hundredth.

≈ 1/100the cost of the research arms behind published tactile-VLA work

One arm is driven by hand. The other copies it, and that’s how every demonstration was recorded.

160 demonstrations, one hand at a time.

Every episode is a person driving a leader arm while the follower copies it, then resetting the scene by hand. Eight cells on a 3×3 grid, exactly 20 episodes each, the noodle rotated through ±90° in 10° steps.

Four rounds around the perimeter, the walk order reversed on rounds 2 and 4, so no cell always sits at the same point in a round.

Two cameras. One goes blind at the worst moment.

The overhead camera frames the workspace at 800×600. At the instant of grasp, the gripper hides the object from it — so a 640×480 camera on the wrist keeps the contact legible. That blind spot is the case for touch.

Two policies, the same 160 episodes.

About 52M parameters

  • ResNet18 vision backbone
  • Predicts 100 actions at a time
  • 42,308 training steps
  • Learning rate 1e-5, constant

About 450M parameters

  • Pretrained vision-language model, vision encoder frozen
  • Predicts 50 actions at a time
  • 20,000 training steps
  • Learning rate 1e-4, warmup then decay

Both trained on one RTX 3060. Both run on a MacBook.

B2

Zero training episodes. Both policies succeeded.

A policy that memorized its training trajectories and one that learned the task can look identical on the cells they trained on. The clean test is to put the object somewhere it has never been. B2 has trained cells on all four sides and none inside, so it tests whether the policy can interpolate.

Swipe for more runs

The servos already report load.

Every STS3215 reports its own load. Pressed by hand on the bench, peaks ran from 48 to 152 by joint. That makes it a candidate touch signal with no added hardware.

It isn’t in either policy yet. The readings come in steps of 4, light contact reads exactly zero, and every value includes pose-dependent static torque, not contact alone. Phase 3 wires it in on a contact-rich insertion task — with a go/no-go on whether touch actually beats vision.

What this doesn’t show.

  • Nothing about touch. Both policies are vision-only; no tactile channel was recorded, trained on, or evaluated.
